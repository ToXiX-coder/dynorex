# Modified-car history records: underserved, not uncontested

A history record for modified cars is a genuinely **underserved** space in the US, but it is **not uncontested**. No mainstream vehicle history report records aftermarket modifications, and owner-entered records never reach the Carfax report. Edmunds will not price modified cars at all, and instant-offer platforms reduce a build to a yes/no "modified?" checkbox that discounts the car. Against that, the basic product (log your mods, attach receipts, share a link, hand the record to the buyer) already exists in at least five small apps: BuildSheet, CarBuilds, My Garage Journal, CarJourney and Auto ModList, plus UK entrants such as Custodian and ModMap. None of them shows meaningful scale or funding, and none found combines installer attribution confirmed by the shop, measured results tied to an install, and distribution into the places where modified cars are actually sold. That combination is the open ground, and Dynorex already has most of the owner-side pieces. The strongest wedge is the **sale moment for enthusiast private and auction sales**, where sellers already assemble receipts and mod lists by hand, with **insurance documentation** as the second use. The biggest risks are not Carfax copying the idea. They are shops refusing to put their name on emissions-relevant work, buyers discounting owner-entered records as easy to fake, and any marketing that sounds like a guarantee. The founder's rule against ever saying "verified" turns out to be a legal and trust asset, not a limitation. Evidence on demand is thinner than the market-size evidence. No Reddit sentiment could be retrieved, no survey measures how many owners keep build records, and no dataset compares documented with undocumented builds at auction.

## The data gap is structural: Carfax cannot see most modifications

Carfax builds its reports from third-party feeds: DMVs, salvage and title agencies, insurers, service shops, auctions, fleets and police. Its brand credibility rests on owners not being able to edit their own reports ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carfax,_Inc.); [Quora](https://www.quora.com/Are-car-owners-allowed-to-modify-their-vehicle-history-reports-on-Carfax)). Its consumer Car Care app lets owners log DIY work and attach receipts, but Carfax support told a Rennlist user in 2020 that "this information will not be included on the CARFAX Vehicle History Report" ([Rennlist](https://rennlist.com/forums/997-forum/873378-my-carfax-app-for-diy-folks.html)). Shop-installed mods can reach Carfax through shop-management software. **Tekmetric's integration, however, sends only the job title, which Carfax then "qualitatively interpret[s]/categorize[s]" into its own standard service descriptions** ([Tekmetric Support](https://support.tekmetric.com/hc/en-us/articles/360036301793-CARFAX-Integration-FAQS-Configuration)). Shopmonkey advertises a similar pipe ([Shopmonkey](https://www.shopmonkey.io/integrations/carfax)). The likely result is that a turbo upgrade from a tuning shop shows up as a generic service line, if it shows up at all, and a DIY coilover install never appears. This is an inference from how the integration works; no source tested what a mod-heavy car's Carfax actually shows. Carfax does have a dealer pricing tool, History Impact, that trade press says accounts for aftermarket parts, but it draws only on data already in the report ([Auto Remarketing](https://www.autoremarketing.com/ar/carfaxs-new-history-report-enhancement-geared-specifically-dealers)).

The valuation side has the same blind spot. **Edmunds does not provide True Market Value pricing for vehicles with aftermarket parts** and tells owners that customizations likely reduce trade-in value ([Edmunds Help](https://help.edmunds.com/hc/en-us/articles/206103057-What-is-the-True-Market-Value-on-aftermarket-parts), via search summary because the page returned 403). Carvana's offer tool asks about modifications. One Ford Ranger owner reported an offer of **$28,300 when answering yes versus $33,491 otherwise**, a gap of about $5,200 ([Ranger5G forum](https://www.ranger5g.com/forum/goto/post?id=255753)). That is a single, undated anecdote. Even so, it shows the pattern: the instant-offer channel has no way to tell good, documented work from a liability, so it discounts all of it.

The market then splits in two. In dealer and wholesale channels, mods are worth roughly nothing and often less than nothing ([Motor1](https://www.motor1.com/news/792414/souping-up-car-changes-value/)). In private and enthusiast-auction channels, well-executed builds can sell well above stock guide values. Jalopnik described a heavily built 2008 Forester selling on Bring a Trailer for **$29,000 against a $7,750 NADA value** ([Jalopnik](https://jalopnik.com/datsun-260z-ford-excursion-ferrari-328-the-biggest-s-1848737028)). Hagerty data, as reported by Motorious, shows LS3 restomod Chevelles selling above guide while stock examples fell ([Motorious](https://www.motorious.com/articles/restomod/this-supercharged-ls3-chevelle-restomod-shows-where-the-market-is-really-headed/)). Hagerty's price guide says documentation supporting build claims can greatly affect value ([Hagerty](https://hagerty.ca/valuationtools/About-our-prices)). Documentation is what lets a seller reach the second kind of price. No source quantified the documentation premium, so treat it as well-motivated but unmeasured.

## Five small apps already offer logging and transfer, but not attributed trust

The "transfer the build to the next owner" mechanic is not novel. The table below summarizes the closest products, all from vendor pages and none tested hands-on.

| Product | Focus | Transfer / share | Installer or shop confirmation | Measured results | Price / scale signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BuildSheet (US, Charlotte) | Mods + maintenance, part numbers, vendor, mileage | Unique URL + QR; claims history stays with the car; compares itself to Carfax | None described; no installer field | Not described | Free, "very much in beta" |
| CarBuilds (Hungary) | Mod builds, iOS/Android | Full transfer, branded "Verified Build History" (mechanism unexplained) | None described | Not described | Pro $14.99/mo; early-access page showed 131 of 200 spots taken |
| My Garage Journal | Maintenance, invoices | Claim-code transfer, seller keeps read-only archive, PDF sale report | "Verified" means evidence attached, not shop-confirmed | No | Free or €29.99/yr |
| CarJourney | Mods + maintenance, AI receipt scanning | Shareable build profile, export | Not described | Not described | Free tier |
| Custodian (UK) | Mod log with install type, cost, stat impact | PDF brochure | Not described | "Stat impact" field | Insurance-linked; no user numbers |

Sources: [BuildSheet](https://buildsheet.io/); [CarBuilds](https://www.carbuilds.com/); [My Garage Journal](https://www.mygaragejournal.com/); [CarJourney](https://carjourney.io/blog/best-app-tracking-car-modifications); [Custodian](https://www.custodian.club/news/modifications-is-officially-live).

Several more sit on the edges. Auto ModList offers shareable build pages. MotorMia is an AI modding assistant that tracks dyno results; its App Store listing showed **3.6 stars from just 14 ratings** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/mia-ai-car-modding-assistant/id6737239101)). ModMap (UK) is in closed beta with a "verified ownership record" as a stated goal ([ModMap](https://modmap.co.uk/)). **BuildSheet is the closest competitor on positioning** because it pitches itself directly against Carfax. **My Garage Journal is the closest on transfer mechanics.** Neither appears to capture who installed a part in a way the installer confirms.

Two points matter for Dynorex. First, the category is unconsolidated: beta labels, a 200-seat early-access program and no funding found suggest nobody has won, and the notes did not check Crunchbase or App Store volumes, so this is inferred. Second, rivals are already diluting the word "verified." CarBuilds and My Garage Journal both use it for things that are not independent confirmation. Dynorex's rule of labeling provenance precisely (who entered it, what evidence is attached, who confirmed it) rather than claiming verification is a real point of difference, provided it is made visible to buyers. Dynorex's existing features (receipts, odometer log, "fitted by," shareable history link, dyno sheet photos, insurance mod list) already match or exceed every product in the table on the owner side. **What it lacks relative to the open ground is the counterparty: a shop confirming its own install, and a buyer or platform consuming the record.**

The adjacent players are not competitors yet. Hagerty requires four photos for modified vehicles, usually no appraisal, and tells owners to "keep careful documentation of any work," but it stores nothing that transfers ([Hagerty FAQ](https://www.hagerty.com/insurance/frequently-asked-questions/general-guidelines); [Hagerty](https://www.hagerty.com/media/buying-and-selling/valuating-your-collector-car/)). Cobb Accessports are "married" to a VIN, which creates an implicit tune-install record, but no owner- or buyer-visible lookup exists ([Cobb wiki](https://cobbtuning.atlassian.net/wiki/x/ZoBMHQ)). Blockchain history projects such as carVertical and VINchain target mileage, title and accidents, not aftermarket parts ([CoinGeek](https://coingeek.com/volkswagen-partners-carvertical-automotive-blockchain-apps/)).

## A $53B parts market with almost no structured record, but thin direct demand evidence

The supply side is large and well sourced. **US specialty-equipment sales reached $52.92B in 2025**, roughly flat in real terms. **About one in four vehicle owners** bought accessory or performance products, more than half of buyers are under 40, and **pickups took 30% of sales, over $15B** ([SEMA eNews](https://www.sema.org/news-media/enews/2026/28/heres-what-you-need-know-semas-2026-market-report)). The most recent public segment split (2023) shows **$12.56B in engine/performance and $12.38B in wheels, tires and handling**. Those two segments are the relevant core, since floor mats and roof racks do not need a history record. **58% of accessorized vehicles are used for commuting**, so these are mostly daily drivers, not show cars ([The Shop](https://theshopmag.com/news/sema-releases-2024-market-report/)). Hagerty separately counts **about 8.6M insurable modified collector-type vehicles**, whose owners average 59 years old and carry **$48K average insurable value versus $35K for stock owners** ([Hagerty Agent](https://hagertyagent.com/resources/hagerty-insights/the-modified-vehicle-market)). That figure excludes modern tuner cars and lifted trucks, so the true modified population is larger.

The transaction side is where a record gets used. Cox forecasts **38.3M used-vehicle sales in 2026, of which 20.3M are dealer retail** ([NIADA/Cox](https://niada.com/dashboard/used-vehicle-sales-expected-to-dip-in-2026/)). The remaining ~18M includes private-party sales but also other non-retail transactions; that split is a back-calculation, not a Cox figure. The enthusiast auction channel is small by units but rich by value. **Bring a Trailer sold $1.713B across 49,486 auctions in 2025 with 81% sell-through and 1.65M registered users** ([The Shop](https://theshopmag.com/news/bring-a-trailer-record-growth-2025/)). That implies an average sale of roughly $43K. Hagerty insures **2.8M vehicles** and its marketplace moved about **$566M** in private and auction sales in 2025 ([Hagerty](https://newsroom.hagerty.com/press/hagerty-reports-full-year-2025-results-provides-2026-growth-outlook/)).

The demand evidence is directionally consistent but anecdotal. Shop blogs tell buyers "Documentation Is Everything. Ask for receipts" ([Valencia Motorsports](https://valenciamotorsports.com/should-you-buy-that-modified-car-what-to-check-before-you-buy/)). Forum posters say they will not pay for a modified car without history ([AnandTech](https://forums.anandtech.com/threads/how-closely-does-the-average-car-dealer-check-out-a-car-before-accepting-it-as-trade.329584/post-28723395)). A UK buyer told a car was stock found a stage 2 remap in the previous owner's dyno sheets, and their insurance premium rose as a result ([RS246](https://forum.rs246.com/viewtopic.php?p=912346)). BaT sellers already post receipts on listings; one Ranger with $40K+ in mods went up with 200+ photos and receipts ([Ranger5G](https://www.ranger5g.com/forum/goto/post?id=624802)). The consensus is not "never buy modified" but "only buy documented, reputable work," which is the exact job of the product.

The evidence is weak in four places. **No Reddit threads could be retrieved at all**, so no claim about r/cars, r/WRX, r/Miata or r/askcarsales sentiment is supported. No survey measures what share of modifiers keep receipts or spreadsheets. No forum thread explicitly asking for a "Carfax for mods" was found. No BaT or Cars & Bids dataset shows that documented builds sell for more. The case for demand rests on behavior (sellers assembling documentation by hand, insurers asking for it) rather than on stated demand or measured price effects. That is reasonable founder evidence, but it should be tested directly before building heavily.

## Owners will not pay much; the sale and the insurance claim are where money changes hands

Carfax shows where the money is in this category, and it is not consumer reports. **Carfax revenue was $1,142M in 2025, 81% subscription, 85% US**, with 40K+ dealer customers and **92K+ service-shop data partners**. Its consumer Car Care app has **53M+ users and is free**, funded by the business side ([SEC 8-K Ex. 99.1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000064040/000110465926058908/tm2613574d2_ex99-1.htm)). Consumer one-off reports sell for about $30–45, with NMVTIS-based discounters pushing toward $5.99 ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/low-cost-carfax-why-5-99-is-the-right-price-for-vehicle-history-in-2026-trKQIVRr7j0Ef4mZ6lTt); pricing sources are low quality). The "Carfax for houses" attempt, Housefax, moved from $10 reports to agent subscriptions of $89–469/month and never reached scale ([Inman](https://www.inman.com/2015/10/30/housefax-launches-subscription-service-for-home-history-reports/)). DriveTribe, an enthusiast platform with famous backers, shut down in January 2022 after automaker ad budgets collapsed, having lost $16M in its first two years ([The Drive](https://www.thedrive.com/news/43814/drivetribe-is-finally-shutting-down-for-good)). The lesson across these comparisons is that record-keeping alone sustains a free tier, not a business. Revenue comes from the transaction, from B2B subscribers, or from an insurer.

The willingness-to-pay anchors line up with that. Hagerty's **~930K paid Drivers Club members** generate about $82M in "membership and other" revenue, roughly $88 per member ([Hagerty](https://newsroom.hagerty.com/press/hagerty-reports-full-year-2025-results-provides-2026-growth-outlook/)). That is a ceiling for an enthusiast annual fee, not a target. CarBuilds asks $14.99/month and My Garage Journal €29.99/year, with no evidence of how many pay. On the transaction side, BaT buyers pay **5% (minimum $250, cap $7,500)** and sellers pay $99–429 to list ([hypercars.io](https://hypercars.io/blog/bring-a-trailer-fees)). On a ~$43K average sale, a $30–100 seller-paid "build record" export is a rounding error next to the fees, and is easy to justify if it lifts the price or the sell-through. Shops already pay **$199–499/month** for Tekmetric or Shopmonkey ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/190952/Tekmetric/pricing/); [PricingSaaS](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/shopmonkey)), so asking them for another subscription is a hard sell. A free "confirm this install" link that doubles as a shop profile is more realistic.

Insurance is the second paying moment, and arguably the more urgent one for ordinary owners. Progressive's standard coverage reportedly includes only **$1,000 for custom parts, with add-on coverage up to about $5,000**, and owners report payouts limited to items with receipts ([JL Wrangler Forums](https://www.jlwranglerforums.com/forum/goto/post?id=2966400); confirm on Progressive.com). Guidance on custom-parts coverage stresses professional installation invoices and notes that DIY installs may not be covered ([PennyPincher](https://pennypincher.com/articles/insurance/auto/custom-parts-equipment-coverage-car-modifications)). Hagerty tells its agents to collect photos, build receipts and mod lists ([Hagerty Agent](https://www.hagertyagent.com/resources/hagerty-insights/how-to-quote-modified-vehicles-with-confidence)). In the US the common loss is probably **under-recovery on a claim**, not a voided policy. The voided-policy evidence comes from the UK Financial Ombudsman ([FOS](https://www.financial-ombudsman.org.uk/news/car-modifications-can-wreck-insurance)), and no US claims data was found. Dynorex's insurance mod list already addresses this. Whether any insurer would accept or pay for structured records is unverified.

## The real risks are shop reluctance, forged receipts and claim language, not Carfax

**Emissions law is the sharpest risk, and it targets the most valuable feature.** Federal enforcement has softened. DOJ dropped criminal OBD-tampering prosecutions in January 2026, and a June 29, 2026 "Freedom to Fix" memo pushes EPA toward leniency for good-faith individuals ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/environmental-edge/2026/07/new-presidential-memorandum-and-epa-guidance-on-vehicle-tampering)). But civil enforcement continues, the Clean Air Act has a **5-year statute of limitations**, and a future administration could reverse course ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/the-nickel-report/doj-curbs-criminal-prosecutions-for-motor-vehicle-tampering-but-noncompliance-remains-a-costly-proposition)). California is not softening: **CARB fined COBB $1,000,000 in August 2026** ([CARB](https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/cobb-tuning-products-llc-settlement)) and expressly applies its rules to "installers," having settled with an installer shop ([CARB advisory 339](https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/sites/default/files/classic/enf/advs/advs339.pdf); [CARB](https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/mikes-radiator-muffler-service-incorporated-settlement)). A shop confirming "installed DPF-delete tune on this VIN on this date" is creating a dated admission against itself. Shops will avoid confirming emissions-relevant work, and the product should not ask them to. The platform's own exposure looks lower: courts applied Section 230 to dismiss the government's civil defeat-device suit against eBay, and in EZ Lynk ([Eric Goldman](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/?p=26894); [Sidley](https://environmentalenergybrief.sidley.com/?p=5876)). That protection disappears if the platform recommends or sells defeat parts, and it never covers criminal claims. A compliance-aware parts taxonomy (CARB EO number, "off-road only," "unknown") and a "returned to stock" event, which EPA's July 2026 letter identifies as the clearest lawful path, turn this risk into a feature buyers in CARB states would value. Whether to accept records of known-illegal mods at all is a question for counsel.

**Liability for inaccurate records favors exactly the founder's rule.** Carfax has been sued repeatedly over incompleteness. A class settlement forced a prominent "may not be complete" warning ([WardsAuto](https://wardsauto.com/dealers/if-the-fax-don-t-fit-then-can-you-acquit-)), and Georgia's Raysoni decision let a fraud claim proceed when an "as is" disclaimer was undercut by surrounding language ([CVN](https://blog.cvn.com/carfax-report-consumer-fraud-trial-to-begin-in-fulton-county-georgia)). For seller-authored content, Section 230 protected Copart over a seller-supplied value estimate ([Cohen v. Copart](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/?p=24532)). It did not protect a platform's *own* misrepresentations ([Bride v. YOLO](https://epic.org/ninth-circuit-recognizes-section-230-not-a-get-out-of-jail-free-card-for-tech-companies-that-allegedly-lie-to-users/)). A platform-issued "Verified" badge is the platform's own statement, and the most likely way to lose that protection. Labels that attribute rather than certify ("Owner-entered," "Receipt attached," "Confirmed by [shop account] on [date]") keep content in the user's mouth. No court has ruled whether vehicle history reports fall under FCRA. Tying records to named owners and feeding insurers could push the product toward it, so confirm this with a lawyer before any insurer data deal.

**Trust is the product, and receipts are weak evidence.** Carfax counts about **2.14M vehicles on the road with false odometers, up 18.5% since 2021** ([Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2025/01/25/aaron-turpen-odometer-fraud-is-getting-more-commonplace-thanks-to-electronics)), and NHTSA's older estimate is about 450,000 vehicles sold each year with false readings ([NHTSA](https://www.nhtsa.gov/es/seguridad-de-vehiculos/fraude-de-odometro)). Receipt images are easy to forge with current tools. Owner-entered records therefore carry low weight on their own, which is presumably why Carfax keeps DIY entries off its reports. The workable answer is an evidence ladder shown per entry: owner claim, then receipt with upload timestamp and file hash, then part serial or CARB EO, then confirmation from the installing shop's account, then a cross-check against odometer readings from other sources. Add append-only edits with visible history and flags for odometer readings that go backwards. Dynorex's odometer log is a natural base for this.

**The Carfax name is off-limits in public.** Carfax won a TTAB cancellation of AAA's "AAA CarFacts" and has pursued sound-alike domains through UDRP ([VitalLaw](https://www.vitallaw.com/news/trademark-ttab-auto-club-s-aaa-carfacts-mark-cancelled-confusion-with-carfax-likely/ipm016363afd87c2a1000a7d690b11c18cbab09); [ADR Forum](https://www.adrforum.com/DomainDecisions/1479897.htm)). "Carfax for modified cars" is fine in a private pitch. In a product name, domain, ad or tagline it is a liability, and any "-fax" or "car facts" sound-alike is worse. Comparative copy ("Carfax doesn't show your mods") is generally safer, but should be checked with counsel.

**Incumbent copycat risk is real but lower than it looks.** Carfax has every technical building block: 90K+ partner shops, owner logging in Car Care, and new VIN-specific features ([Carfax Service Shops](https://www.carfaxserviceshops.com/); [PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/carfax-report-now-delivers-future-reliability-based-on-a-vehicles-unique-vin-specific-history-302735240.html)). Its incentives, however, point away from publishing owner claims about modified, possibly non-compliant cars to a dealer customer base that discounts mods anyway. The more plausible fast-followers are BaT, Cars & Bids or Hagerty adding a structured "build sheet" to listings, since their sellers already write one in free text. This is unverified; no source on their product plans was found. That threat is also the partnership opportunity: a product that feeds those listings is more valuable to them as a supplier than as a target.

## Conclusion

The honest verdict is that the *idea* is contested and the *execution that matters* is not. Mod logging with a transferable link is close to a commodity, offered free or nearly free by a handful of sub-scale apps. What nobody has built is a record a skeptical buyer, insurer or auction can weigh: installer attribution confirmed by the installer, measured results tied to the install event, compliance status, and an auditable trail, delivered into the listing or claim where it gets used. The thing to defend is the trust model and the distribution, not the data, and Dynorex's "never say verified" rule is the right foundation for both. It keeps the platform on the safe side of Section 230 and separates Dynorex from rivals who are already cheapening the word.

The strongest wedge is the seller at the moment of an enthusiast sale, a person with a ~$43K car, $2K+ of auction fees and a manual chore of assembling receipts. The MVP should be a **"sale-ready build record"** built on what Dynorex already has. It needs a buyer-facing share page where every entry carries its evidence level and edit history. It needs a one-tap, no-account "confirm this install" link for shops, deliberately not offered for emissions-relevant categories, with CARB EO capture and a "returned to stock" event instead. Dyno sheets should be attached to the install they followed. It should export a listing-ready build sheet and PDF for BaT, Cars & Bids and private sales, plus a claim-code transfer to the buyer. Keep owner logging free; charge sellers once at export or listing time; treat the insurance mod list as the retention hook between sales. Before building past that, the cheapest high-value test is to get 20–30 BaT or Cars & Bids sellers of modified cars to use the export, and ask the auction platforms and one specialty insurer whether they would link to it. The three research gaps to close are Reddit and forum sentiment, a scrape of documented-versus-undocumented auction results, and whether tuning shops will put their name on non-emissions installs at all.
